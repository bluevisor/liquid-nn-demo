How a liquid neuron works

An ordinary neuron reacts at one fixed speed. A liquid neuron changes how fast it reacts depending on what it's looking at — it speeds up for a sharp signal and coasts through a slow one. Here's the whole idea, from a leaky bucket up to the equation, with a bench you can drive.

Liquid Time-Constant networks Hasani et al., AAAI 2021 · one neuron, no libraries

Start here

A neuron is a leaky bucket

Before anything liquid, picture the plainest possible neuron as a bucket of water. Input pours in; the bucket also has a hole, so the level always leaks back toward empty. Its state x is just the water level, and it obeys one line of calculus — the leaky integrator:

dxdt = − 1τ x + input

The τ (“tau”) is the time constant — how many seconds the bucket takes to react. Big hole → small τ → snappy. Pinhole → big τ → sluggish. In a normal network τ is baked in at training time and never moves.

That single number is the neuron's whole sense of time, and it's the problem. Pick a small τ and the neuron twitches at every bit of noise. Pick a large τ and it's calm but misses anything sudden. One fixed reaction speed can't be both careful and quick.

The one idea

What if the reaction speed could move?

Liquid neurons make one change, and everything follows from it: let the leak rate depend on the input itself. A small learned gate f(u, x) — a single sigmoid — opens the hole wider when the signal warrants it and closes it when things are quiet:

dxdt = − [ 1τ + f(u,x) ] x + f(u,x) · A

The gate f appears twice: it sets how fast the state decays and how hard it's pulled toward the target A. Because f rides on the live input, the neuron's clock is now a moving part.

Group the two decay terms and you can read off the neuron's real reaction speed at any instant — the quantity that gives these networks their name:

τeff = 1 1 / τ + f(u,x)

The effective time constant. When the gate opens (f large), the denominator grows and τeff shrinks — the neuron reacts faster. When the input is dull, f→0 and it relaxes back to its slow baseline τ. It is “liquid” because this number is never fixed; it flows with the data.

Under the hood

How you actually compute it

There's no closed-form solution — the time constant depends on the state you haven't computed yet. So you step the ODE forward numerically. The trick that makes it stable is a semi-implicit update: evaluate the gate at the current state, but solve for the next state on both sides of the equation. Rearranging the discretized ODE gives a clean division:

xnext = x + Δt · f · A 1 + Δt · (1/τ + f)

The denominator is always > 1, so the state can never blow up no matter how big the step — the update is unconditionally stable. In practice you take a few of these sub-steps per observation to keep accuracy high.

Two properties fall out of this for free, and they're the reason anyone reaches for a liquid neuron in the first place:

Δt is an input

Irregular time is native

The gap between samples, Δt, sits right in the update. Feed data that arrives at uneven intervals — sensors, medical records, event logs — and the neuron integrates over the real elapsed time instead of pretending every step is equal.

τ moves, not the weights

It generalizes across timescales

Because reaction speed is computed on the fly, a liquid net trained at one sampling rate still tracks a signal sampled 2–3× slower — a regime it never saw. A fixed-clock RNN, given the same test, drifts.

In your liquid_demo.py, the LTC network beat a same-size GRU on exactly this test: trained on Δt of 0.10–0.30 s, then evaluated at an unseen 0.45–0.75 s. That's the generalization claim on the right, made concrete — see liquid_vs_gru.png.

In one breath

The whole thing, restated

A liquid neuron is a leaky integrator whose leak rate is itself a learned function of the input. That one move turns a fixed time constant into a τeff that flows with the data: the neuron speeds up for sharp signals, slows for calm ones, handles irregular sampling because Δt is baked into the solver, and generalizes to timescales it never trained on — all with a handful of parameters per neuron.

Everything else in a Liquid Time-Constant network is just wiring many of these neurons together and reading them out. The idea itself is the two boxed equations above, and you just watched it run.
